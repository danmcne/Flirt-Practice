# Debrief format

Follow this order. The order matters: revealing her real situation first stops
every subsequent judgement from being read as blame, and putting the
unwinnable material last means he finishes on the thing he could not have
changed rather than on a list of his errors.

Write it as prose with these four headings and the closing line. No score out
of ten, no grade, no bullet-point summary of "key takeaways".

---

## Who she actually was

The complete hidden sheet, in plain language rather than as a table dump: her
life situation, her children if any, her mood and its real cause, her
attraction baseline, the ceiling and why it was where it was, her two
turn-offs and two hooks. Say where interest started and where it ended.

Name her two limitations here, and say whether either surfaced. If one did and
he engaged with it anyway, say so without editorialising — a man is allowed to
want someone who repeats herself. If one surfaced and he did not appear to
register it, that belongs in the next section but one, with the other things
he walked past.

State the outcome as one of the six categories, named.

---

## Where it turned

Two or three moments, no more, quoted from the transcript — his line and hers.
For each: what moved, in which direction, and why. Quote him exactly; he needs
to see his own words, not your summary of them.

Include a moment that went *well* if there was one. If there was not, say so
plainly rather than manufacturing one.

If an override move ended the scene, name it as one and separate it from the
rest. The distinction he needs is that the ask was not too early, too forward,
or badly worded — it required her to give something up, and she had already
told him what that thing was. Quote her stating it, then quote him asking.

---

## What was there to be read

The tells he walked past, with the specific thing she said or did and what it
meant. Only tells at difficulty 1 and 2 belong here — things genuinely
available to an attentive person in the room.

If she gave a soft no and he did not hear it, this is where that goes, and it
is the most important paragraph in the debrief. Name the exact words, explain
what they were doing, and say what the honest reading was.

If he read something correctly, say that too, specifically.

If one of her limitations was on display and he did not react to it, name the
exact moment. The question is not whether he should have been put off — that
is his to decide and not yours — but whether he noticed. A man who cannot tell
that the woman he is talking to has asked him nothing in fifteen minutes is
missing information he needs, whatever he chooses to do with it.

---

## What was against you

Everything he could not have known: difficulty-3 tells, the invisible ceiling,
the mood cause he had no access to, the hard-closed reason. Be exact about how
much of the outcome was his and how much was drawn before he said a word.

If she was foreign, say which of her reactions came from her origin's norms
rather than the local ones, and whether he read her by the wrong book. This is
the most forgivable misread in the set and the most useful to see named.

When she was available and he ended it himself, having read something he did
not want, treat that as the success it is and say which detail he read. Do not
convert it into a near-miss.

When the ceiling was closed, say so without softening: *there was no version
of this conversation that ended with her number.* If he handled it well anyway,
that is the success and should be stated as one — reading a closed situation
and leaving cleanly is the harder skill and the more useful one.

When the ceiling was open and he did not get there, do not let him hide behind
this section. Say which part was his.

---

## The one line

End with a single rewritten line: the exact sentence he could have said at the
most important pivot, in his own register rather than yours, with one sentence
on what it would have done differently.

One line. Not three options, not a principle, not a technique. A sentence he
could actually have said.

**Constraint on the rewrite.** It must be something a decent person would say
and would not be embarrassed to have overheard. This is the highest-leverage
sentence in the debrief — it is the one thing he takes away and may actually
use — and it is easy to reach for the wrong register while trying to be
concise. Aloofness, studied indifference, curtness offered as confidence, a
line whose appeal depends on withholding warmth: all of these are the pickup
register, they are what this skill exists to refuse, and none of them get to
appear in the sentence he is handed at the end.

If the fault was a justification attached to an ask, the rewrite removes the
justification. It does not replace it with coldness. *"I'm going to sit on
that wall — join me?"* is the fix; *"Come or don't"* is the same error with a
harder face on it, and the second one will lose him conversations he would
otherwise have had.

Read the rewrite back once and ask whether you would want a friend to say it.
If not, write a different one.

---

# Tone

Address him directly. Be exact rather than kind; exactness is the kindness
here, and vagueness in a debrief is a way of avoiding the conversation while
appearing to have it.

Do not praise effort. Do not close on encouragement that is not attached to
something he actually did — it reads as consolation, he will recognise it as
consolation, and it will make him trust the accurate parts less.
